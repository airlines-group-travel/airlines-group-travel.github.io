The single biggest difference between booking ten seats and booking one is that
you do not need to know who is travelling yet. That one difference is what a
group contract buys you, and almost every other rule follows from it.

## What a deposit actually secures

When an airline's group desk quotes a fare, the quote comes with a deposit and
an expiry. Paying the deposit converts a quote into held inventory: a specific
number of seats, on specific flights, at a fixed price, until a stated date.

Two things are worth understanding about that price. It is fixed against fare
increases — if the public fare doubles because the route fills up, your held
seats do not move. It is also fixed against decreases, which is the trade. You
are buying certainty, and certainty costs the possibility of a bargain later.

Deposits are commonly a per-seat amount rather than a percentage, and on most
carriers they are non-refundable but transferable within the same booking. The
practical consequence: reducing your group size before the final deadline
usually forfeits the deposit on the seats you drop, while changing *who* sits in
them usually does not.

## The three dates that matter

Every group contract has the same three dates, and they are the only ones worth
tracking closely.

**The deposit deadline.** Until this passes, you have a quote, not a booking.
Miss it and the seats return to inventory at whatever the fare is that day.

**The name deadline.** This is the date the airline needs the full passenger
list — legal names as they appear on passports, matched exactly. It typically
falls somewhere between three and eight weeks before departure, and it varies
more between carriers than any other term.

**The final payment date.** Usually at or shortly after the name deadline.
Tickets are issued once this clears.

Anything you want to change is cheap before the name deadline and expensive
after it. That is the whole shape of the thing.

## Name changes after ticketing

Once tickets are issued, a group booking behaves much more like a set of
individual tickets. Most airlines still allow a name change — that is the point
of a group fare — but it moves from free to a fee per change, and some carriers
cap how many they will accept.

If your group is genuinely uncertain, that uncertainty has a price and it is
worth pricing it deliberately. Ask what a post-ticketing name change costs
*before* you accept a quote, not after. Two fares that look identical can differ
by a few hundred dollars in change fees across a group of forty.

## Where groups most often lose money

In our experience three things account for most of it, and all three are
avoidable.

Booking too late, so the group fare is quoted against an already-full aircraft.
Group inventory is finite and released early; the same flight quoted four months
out and four weeks out is a different conversation.

Committing to a headcount that was optimistic. It is almost always cheaper to
contract for the number you are confident of and add seats later at the
prevailing fare than to contract high and drop seats against a non-refundable
deposit.

Missing the name deadline by a few days. It sounds trivial. It is the single
most common cause of a group being re-priced.

## Before you ask for a quote

Have four things ready and the quote comes back faster and more accurately: the
route, the date range and how flexible it is, the number of passengers you are
confident about, and the cabin. You do not need names, passport numbers or
payment at this stage — and any agency asking for them before you have seen
terms is doing it in the wrong order.
